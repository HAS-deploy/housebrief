Checking permits before buying

Why Permit History Matters Before You Buy

When you fall in love with a house, the last thing you want is to discover that the beautiful finished basement or the backyard deck was built without a permit. Unpermitted work isn't just a bureaucratic nuisance — it can mean structural problems that were never inspected, insurance claims that get denied, and a renegotiation or collapse of your sale. Checking permits before you close is one of the most concrete forms of due diligence available to a buyer, and it takes far less effort than most people expect.

What Permits Cover and Why They Get Skipped

Permits are required for work that affects the structure, safety systems, or habitable space of a building. That typically includes:

  • Electrical panel upgrades and new circuits
  • Plumbing additions or reroutes
  • HVAC installation or significant modifications
  • Structural changes (removing walls, adding beams, cutting new openings)
  • Room additions, garage conversions, and ADU construction
  • Deck and patio cover construction above a certain square footage
  • Basement or attic finishing that creates living space

Sellers skip permits for several reasons: the work was done by unlicensed contractors, they wanted to avoid inspection delays, they didn't know a permit was required, or they deliberately tried to avoid a property reassessment. None of those reasons become your problem until you own the property.

How to Pull the Permit History Yourself

Start with the Local Building Department

Permit records are public documents. In most jurisdictions they're held by the city or county building department — sometimes called the Department of Building and Safety, the Bureau of Development Services, or simply the Building Division. The name varies but the function is the same.

You have three practical ways to access records:

Online portal. Many mid-size and larger cities have searchable permit databases. Search for "[city name] building permit records" or "[city name] permit portal." You'll typically search by address. The results will show each permit pulled, the type of work described, the contractor's name, the date issued, and — critically — whether it was finaled (meaning the work passed its inspections).

In-person or phone request. If the city doesn't have an online database, call the building department directly and request a permit history for the property address. Most jurisdictions will pull this for free or for a nominal fee. Bring or provide the parcel number (APN) if you have it — this speeds things up considerably.

County assessor records. Cross-reference what the assessor has on file for the property's square footage and room count against what the MLS listing shows. If the assessor says 1,400 square feet and the listing says 1,800, that 400-square-foot gap warrants a direct question to the seller about when that space was added and whether it was permitted.

What to Look For in the Records

A clean permit history will show a permit issued, followed by inspection approvals at key stages, followed by a final sign-off. What you're watching for:

  • Open permits with no final. A permit was pulled but the work was never finaled. This means inspectors never confirmed the work was completed correctly. You'd be buying a property with unresolved code issues.
  • Gaps between what's described and what exists. If you're standing in a finished, tiled bathroom in the basement but see zero plumbing permits in the last 20 years, that's a red flag.
  • Work that exceeds what was permitted. Sometimes a permit was pulled for a minor repair but the actual scope was much larger. The permit record won't show this — your inspector will need to flag it on-site.
  • Permits pulled but not by a licensed contractor. In many jurisdictions, homeowners can pull their own permits for certain work, but the work must still pass inspection. If it was owner-pulled and never finaled, the quality is unknown.

Using Your Home Inspector Effectively

A permit check is not a substitute for a home inspection — they work together. Share whatever permit history you've gathered with your inspector before they go on-site. Ask them specifically to note any work that appears to have been done without permits.

Inspectors are not code enforcement officers and won't declare something illegal, but a good inspector will write observations like "finished basement space shows no evidence of permit record" or "electrical subpanel appears to be a recent addition; recommend verifying permit status." Those observations give you a documented basis for negotiating repairs or a price reduction.

When the Records Reveal a Problem

Unpermitted Work Scenarios and Your Options

If you find unpermitted work, you have real options — this doesn't automatically kill a deal.

Ask the seller to permit and final the work. For work that's relatively recent and likely meets current code, some sellers will agree to open a permit, schedule inspections, and get a final before close. This is cleanest but takes time.

Negotiate a price reduction. Get a contractor's estimate for what it would cost to bring the work up to code and get it legalized. Use that number as the basis for a credit or price reduction. If legalization would require tearing out finished surfaces to expose framing, electrical, or plumbing for inspection, the costs can be significant.

Request a seller disclosure acknowledgment. Even if you decide to proceed as-is, get it in writing that the seller disclosed the unpermitted work. This matters if problems surface later.

Walk away. For major unpermitted work — a full room addition, a garage conversion, a full basement finish — the liability can be substantial enough that walking away is the right call, especially if the seller won't negotiate.

Lender and Insurance Complications

Be aware that certain loan types have strict requirements. FHA and VA loans, in particular, require that the home meet specific standards, and unpermitted additions can trigger appraisal issues. Appraisers will sometimes exclude unpermitted square footage from the livable area calculation, which can directly affect the appraised value and, consequently, how much you can borrow.

Homeowners insurance can also become complicated. If a fire starts in unpermitted electrical work, some insurers will reduce or deny the claim because the work wasn't inspected. Document everything you find and disclose it to your insurance agent when you're shopping coverage.

Common Mistakes Buyers Make

Relying solely on the seller's disclosure. Sellers don't always know what was permitted — previous owners may have done the work. And some sellers know and don't tell you. Verify independently.

Assuming small work doesn't matter. A new electrical circuit for a hot tub, a gas line for a generator, a structural beam — these feel minor but can have serious safety and code implications.

Not checking for permits on recent work. If the listing mentions a "newly renovated kitchen" or "recently added bedroom," those are exactly the items to trace in the permit database. Recent work by motivated sellers wanting to maximize sale price is a high-frequency source of unpermitted additions.

Waiting until after inspection to pull permits. Do this early — ideally as soon as you're seriously considering making an offer, or at minimum immediately after acceptance. You want time to research, ask questions, and negotiate before contingency deadlines expire.

A Practical Timeline

  • Before making an offer: Search the online permit portal if available. Takes 15 minutes.
  • Immediately after acceptance: Request full permit history from building department if not available online.
  • Before your home inspection: Share permit records with your inspector.
  • During inspection contingency period: Use findings to negotiate or request corrective action.
  • Before removing contingencies: Confirm any open permits have been resolved or that you've accounted for the cost.

FAQ

Is permit history always publicly available? In the United States, permit records are generally public records. The method of access varies — some jurisdictions have robust online databases, others require an in-person visit or phone call — but the records are available to anyone, not just the property owner.

What if the city has no records at all for a very old property? Older homes often have little or no permit history simply because permit requirements weren't as comprehensive decades ago. Absent records for a 1940s home aren't automatically a red flag. What you're watching for is modern-looking work — a recently finished basement, a 200-amp panel replacement — with no corresponding permits.

Can unpermitted work be legalized after I buy? Yes. Most jurisdictions have a process for retroactive permitting. You open a permit, inspectors review the work, and if it passes (or once corrections are made), it gets finaled. The challenge is that inspectors often require opening walls or ceilings to verify hidden work. Budget for this possibility if you're buying a property with known unpermitted additions.

Does a real estate agent check permits for me? Not typically as a formal service. Your agent can help you identify what to look for and may flag obvious concerns, but the permit research is your responsibility as a buyer. Don't assume it's being done by someone else.

Does every state or country handle this the same way? No. Requirements vary by jurisdiction. In some rural counties, permitting requirements are minimal or nonexistent. In dense urban areas, permit requirements are comprehensive. Research what's required in the specific jurisdiction where the property sits.
