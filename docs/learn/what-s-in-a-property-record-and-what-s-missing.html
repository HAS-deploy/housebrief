What's in a property record (and what's missing)

What a Property Record Actually Contains

When you pull up a property record — whether through a county assessor's website, a title company portal, or a public records database — you're looking at a document that was built for tax and legal purposes, not for buyers, sellers, or curious neighbors. Understanding that original intent explains both what's reliable in the record and what's conspicuously absent.

The Core Fields You Can Count On

Legal description and parcel identifier

Every property record anchors to a legal description — a formal narrative or coordinate-based description of the land's boundaries, usually drawn from the original survey plat. Alongside that sits the Assessor's Parcel Number (APN), sometimes called a parcel ID or tax map number depending on the county. This number is the key that links the parcel to everything else: tax bills, permits, recorded deeds. If you're cross-referencing records from multiple sources, the APN is the most reliable common denominator.

Ownership information

The record shows the current vested owner — the name or entity on the most recently recorded deed. In most counties this updates within a few weeks of a sale closing, though some rural or understaffed offices can lag by months. One important nuance: the owner of record is whoever held title at the last deed recording. If someone inherited the property informally, or if a trust or LLC owns it, the human being you actually want to reach may not appear anywhere in this field.

Assessed value and tax information

The assessed value is the county's official valuation for tax purposes. In many states this bears little resemblance to market value — California's Proposition 13, for instance, caps annual assessment increases at 2%, meaning a house bought in 1985 may carry an assessed value a fraction of its current market worth. The record will also show the tax rate area, the most recent tax bill amounts, and whether taxes are current or delinquent. Tax delinquency is one of the most useful signals in a property record; it often surfaces distressed ownership situations before they become public knowledge.

Physical characteristics

This section lists the assessor's data on the structure itself: square footage, lot size, number of bedrooms and bathrooms, year built, construction type, and sometimes garage spaces or pool presence. This data is collected for mass appraisal purposes and is often accurate enough to be useful — but it's also frequently stale. If a previous owner finished a basement without a permit, added a bathroom, or converted a garage, that change may or may not have been reported to the assessor. Treat square footage in a property record as a starting point, not a fact.

Recorded deeds and transfer history

Most county records include a chain of title going back years or decades — each deed recorded, the grantor and grantee, the date, and the document number. The recorded sales price may or may not appear, depending on the state. A handful of states (including Texas, Utah, and Alaska) are "non-disclosure" states where sale prices aren't publicly filed. In those states, you'll see transfer dates but no dollar amounts.

What's Typically Missing

This is where people get tripped up, especially when they're trying to make a real decision based on a property record.

Liens That Haven't Been Recorded

A property record reflects what has been filed with the county recorder — but not all liens arrive promptly. A contractor who finished work last month may have a valid mechanic's lien right in progress that hasn't been recorded yet. Federal tax liens sometimes take weeks to appear at the county level after the IRS files them. A preliminary title search catches most of this, but a property record snapshot in time will miss anything in transit.

HOA Status and Fees

Whether a property sits within a homeowners association, what the monthly or annual dues are, whether there are special assessments, or whether the current owner is delinquent on HOA payments — none of this lives in the county property record. You generally have to contact the HOA directly or request disclosure from the seller.

Permit History (Sometimes)

Permit history is a separate county department from the assessor in most jurisdictions. Some counties have integrated databases where you can move from the property record to permit history in a single click. Others require a completely separate search with the building department. And even a thorough permit search won't reveal unpermitted work — by definition, that work was never filed.

Environmental Issues

Whether a property sits on or near a contaminated site, has underground storage tanks, has had soil or groundwater testing, or falls within a specific environmental overlay zone — this is scattered across EPA databases, state environmental agency records, local fire department files, and seller disclosures. A property record tells you nothing about any of it.

Easements and CC&Rs in Full Detail

The property record may note that easements or covenants exist — but it typically just references recorded document numbers rather than spelling out their terms. To understand what a utility easement actually restricts, or what a set of CC&Rs actually prohibits, you need to pull the underlying recorded documents, which are often held at the county recorder's office separately from the assessor's database.

Actual Condition

A property record doesn't know the roof is 25 years old, the HVAC was replaced in 2019, the foundation has a crack, or the basement floods. Physical condition lives in inspection reports, disclosure statements, and your own eyes — not in any public record.

How Records Vary by County and State

Property record quality is wildly inconsistent across jurisdictions. A large metro county like Los Angeles or Cook County (Chicago) has invested heavily in digitized, searchable records going back decades, with high-resolution scanned documents available online. A small rural county might have records maintained in a paper ledger that requires a phone call or in-person visit to access — and even then, accuracy depends on how diligently the local assessor's office has been maintained over the years.

State law also shapes what's in the record. Some states require recording the full consideration (sale price) on the deed, making transfer history rich with price data. Others don't. Some states require recording of certain disclosures; others leave disclosure entirely to private transaction documents that never become public.

A Practical Checklist When You Pull a Property Record

Before drawing conclusions from any property record, run through these quick checks:

  • Confirm the APN matches the address — transcription errors are common, especially on corner lots or properties with recent address changes.
  • Check the last deed date — if it was recorded in the last 90 days, there may be information that hasn't propagated to all databases yet.
  • Note the assessed value year — some counties update annually, others on a lag. A value from three years ago in a volatile market tells you very little.
  • Look for any noted encumbrances — the record may flag that liens or easements exist even if it doesn't detail them.
  • Cross-reference the square footage — compare the record's figure to any listing data and flag discrepancies larger than 10% for follow-up.
  • Check tax status — current vs. delinquent, and how many cycles.

The Gap Between the Record and Reality

The most useful way to think about a property record is as a skeleton. It gives you the bones of a property's legal and tax history — who owns it, what it was worth for tax purposes, when it last sold, and what the county thinks is sitting on the lot. That's genuinely valuable information, especially for comparing properties at scale or establishing a baseline before doing deeper research.

But the flesh — condition, encumbrances, neighbor relationships, environmental context, unpermitted additions — doesn't live in the record. It lives in inspections, disclosures, title searches, HOA documents, and conversations with the people who actually know the property.

Anyone using property records seriously learns to treat them as a first stop, not a final answer.

Frequently Asked Questions

How current is the ownership information in a property record? It depends on how quickly the county recorder processes deeds and how often the assessor's database syncs with recorder filings. In busy metro counties, ownership often updates within two to four weeks of recording. In smaller counties, it can take months.

Can I find out what someone paid for a house from the property record? In most states, yes — the sale price is recorded on the deed or a separate transfer document. In non-disclosure states (Texas, Utah, Alaska, and about a dozen others), sale prices aren't part of the public record.

Why does the square footage in the property record differ from the listing? The assessor measures heated/conditioned living area using their own methodology, which may exclude finished basements, enclosed porches, or additions that weren't permitted. Listing agents sometimes use a different measurement standard or include spaces the assessor doesn't count.

Is a property record the same as a title search? No. A title search is a professional examination of the chain of title, recorded liens, easements, and encumbrances, conducted by a title company or attorney. A property record is raw public data — a title search interprets and verifies it.

What if the property record has wrong information? Contact the county assessor's office directly. Most have a formal appeals or correction process. For factual errors like wrong square footage, you'll typically need documentation — a prior appraisal, permit records, or a survey.
