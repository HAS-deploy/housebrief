Pre-offer due diligence in 30 minutes

Why 30 Minutes Is Enough — If You Use Them Right

Most buyers waste their pre-offer research time looking at the wrong things: scrolling listing photos, checking the school ratings they already know, reading a neighbourhood Wikipedia page. A focused 30 minutes on the right sources gives you enough to write a confident offer, flag dealbreakers before you're emotionally committed, and ask the right questions in negotiation. Here's how to structure it.

The 30-Minute Framework

Split your session into four blocks. Do them in order — each one narrows the scope for the next.

Block 1: Title and Ownership History (8 minutes)

Start at your county recorder or assessor's website. Search the property address and confirm:

  • Who actually owns it. The seller on the listing should match the record owner. A mismatch (estate sale listed under a deceased owner's name, LLC ownership not disclosed) isn't necessarily a problem, but you need to know before drafting.
  • How long they've owned it. A seller who bought 18 months ago and is already listing may have a benign reason (job relocation) or a property-specific one (undisclosed problem). Note it; ask about it.
  • Any liens or encumbrances. Many county sites show deed of trust records. Multiple mortgages, mechanic's liens, or a notice of default are visible here. A title company will do a full search in escrow, but spotting a red flag now costs you nothing.
  • Last sale price. Compare it to the current list price and account for time. A property that sold for significantly more two years ago during peak market conditions is different from one that sold for more last year with no apparent improvements.

If your county site is clunky, try the state's public land records portal or a free property data aggregator — most pull from the same recorder data.

Block 2: Permit History and Zoning (7 minutes)

Go to your city or county's building department portal. Search by address for permit history.

What you're looking for:

  • Unpermitted work. A finished basement, an added bathroom, or a converted garage that appears in listing photos but shows no corresponding permit is a liability you'd be buying. Lenders sometimes won't finance it; insurers won't cover it; and if you ever sell, you inherit the disclosure problem.
  • Open permits. Permits pulled but never finaled mean an inspector never signed off. That work may not meet code. Closing with an open permit is possible but complicated — title companies often require them resolved or escrowed.
  • Recent major work. A new roof with a permit from last year is reassuring. A roof that looks new in photos with no permit is a question mark.

While you're in the building department portal, confirm the zoning designation and cross-check it against how the property is being used. An "office" zoning on what's listed as a single-family home matters. An ADU that isn't permitted as a dwelling matters more.

Block 3: Flood, Environmental, and Hazard Data (7 minutes)

Flood zone: Go to FEMA's Flood Map Service Center (msc.fema.gov) and enter the address. A property in Zone AE or VE requires flood insurance, which can add $1,000–$4,000+ annually to carrying costs. Factor that into your offer math immediately. Zone X is lower risk but doesn't mean zero risk — check if it's shaded (moderate risk) or unshaded.

Wildfire and other hazards: State agencies in California, Colorado, Oregon, and other fire-prone states publish wildfire hazard severity zone maps. A "High" or "Very High" designation affects insurance availability and cost — in some California zip codes, standard carriers have pulled out entirely, and surplus lines coverage runs two to three times the premium.

Environmental records: The EPA's ECHO database and many state environmental agencies publish records of nearby industrial sites, underground storage tanks, and contamination cleanups. If you're buying near an old gas station, dry cleaner, or industrial property, spend two minutes searching. You're not doing a Phase I environmental assessment here — you're checking whether there's anything obviously on the record that your buyer's agent may not have flagged.

Block 4: Comparable Sales and Days on Market (8 minutes)

Pull up public MLS data (Redfin and Zillow both show recent sold prices) and look at:

  • Closed sales in the past 90 days within half a mile, similar square footage and bed/bath count. You're not writing an appraisal — you're sanity-checking the list price.
  • Price reductions on this listing. Most portals show listing history. A property that started $75,000 higher three months ago and has had two reductions tells a different story than a fresh listing.
  • Days on market. In a balanced market, 45+ days without reduction suggests something is wrong with either the price or the property. In a hot market, anything over 10–14 days warrants a question.
  • Relisted properties. If the listing went "active" again after going "pending," a deal fell apart. That happens for buyer financing reasons, but it also happens because an inspection revealed problems. It's worth asking your agent to find out why.

Common Pitfalls in Quick Due Diligence

Mistaking listing data for fact. Square footage on listings comes from various sources — tax records, seller estimates, previous appraisals. Tax records are notoriously wrong by 5–10%. Don't build your offer math around a listed square footage you haven't verified.

Ignoring the HOA. If the property is in a homeowners association, the 30-minute window should include a quick check for HOA litigation (publicly searchable in many states), pending special assessments, and the monthly dues. A $600/month HOA fee changes your debt-to-income ratio and your carrying cost model.

Treating this as a substitute for inspection. Pre-offer due diligence tells you whether it's worth making an offer, not whether the property is sound. A proper inspection — and depending on the property, specialist inspections for sewer, foundation, roof, or environmental issues — happens after your offer is accepted. Don't skip those.

Skipping the satellite and street view check. Spend 60 seconds on Google Maps satellite view and Street View. What's directly behind the property? How close is the commercial building, the highway, or the power line substation? How does the neighborhood actually look at street level versus the curated photos?

What to Do With What You Find

Most of what surfaces in 30 minutes falls into three categories:

1. Confirmed dealbreakers — unpermitted major additions the seller won't credit for, flood zone with uninsurable premiums, active environmental contamination notice. Walk away, or get more answers before proceeding.

2. Negotiating leverage — price reductions, long days on market, liens that need payoff, open permits. These are not reasons to walk; they're reasons to negotiate price or terms.

3. Verification items — things that need a follow-up question or inspection focus. A permitted addition that looks old, recent roof permit from an unknown contractor, HOA with a history of special assessments. Put these on your inspection list and disclosure request.

Write these down before you make the offer. Your agent can incorporate them into your offer terms (inspection contingency language, specific disclosure requests) or your negotiating position.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I do this without a real estate agent? Yes. All the sources listed — county recorder, building department, FEMA, EPA — are public. An agent helps with MLS access and contract mechanics, but the research is independent of agent representation.

What if the county website is outdated or incomplete? Call the building department directly. They can usually tell you in five minutes whether permits were pulled and finaled. It's faster than you'd expect and worth the call for any major work visible in photos.

Is 30 minutes really enough for a commercial property? No. Commercial due diligence involves environmental assessments, zoning use analysis, rent rolls, lease abstracts, and financial underwriting. The 30-minute framework applies to residential purchases.

What if I find a lien but the seller says it'll be paid at closing? That's standard — mortgages are paid off from sale proceeds. Judgment liens and mechanic's liens should also be resolved at closing, but confirm with your title company that they'll require payoff, not just acknowledgment.

Do I need to repeat this process after my offer is accepted? Your escrow period includes formal title search, inspection, and disclosure review — all of which go deeper. The pre-offer session is a filter, not a final check. Think of it as answering "should I proceed?" not "is this property perfect?"
