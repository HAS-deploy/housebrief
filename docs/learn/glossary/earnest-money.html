earnest money — home-buying glossary

What it means

Earnest money is a deposit a buyer puts down shortly after a seller accepts their offer, signaling that they're serious about completing the purchase. It typically ranges from 1% to 3% of the home's purchase price, though the amount is negotiable and can vary by local market. The funds are held in escrow by a neutral third party — usually a title company or escrow agent — until closing.

If the deal closes successfully, the earnest money is applied toward the buyer's down payment or closing costs. If the deal falls apart, who keeps the money depends on why it fell apart and what the purchase contract says.

Why it matters

Earnest money protects the seller. If a buyer backs out for a reason not covered by a contingency in the contract — say, they simply change their mind — the seller can keep the deposit as compensation for taking the home off the market. For buyers, it creates real financial stakes, so it's critical to understand under what circumstances you can get your money back. Most standard contracts include contingencies (inspection, financing, appraisal) that allow buyers to exit and reclaim their deposit if something specific goes wrong. If you waive those contingencies to make a competitive offer, you risk losing your earnest money if the deal collapses.

Example

A buyer makes an offer on a $400,000 home and submits $6,000 (1.5%) in earnest money within three days of acceptance. The purchase contract includes an inspection contingency. The home inspection reveals major foundation issues, the buyer invokes the contingency, and the deal is cancelled. The escrow agent returns the full $6,000 to the buyer. Had the buyer waived the inspection contingency and then walked away after seeing the inspection report, the seller could have claimed the $6,000.

Common confusions

Earnest money vs. down payment: These are not the same thing. The down payment is the large sum you bring to closing; earnest money is just a smaller good-faith deposit made upfront. At closing, earnest money is typically credited toward the down payment — it doesn't come on top of it.

Earnest money vs. option fee: In some states (Texas, for example), buyers pay a separate, smaller "option fee" to get an unrestricted right to back out during an option period. The option fee is usually non-refundable by design; earnest money is refundable under the right contingencies. The two can exist in the same transaction.

Thinking it's automatically refundable: Many buyers assume they'll always get it back if something goes wrong. Whether you're protected depends entirely on the contingencies written into your specific contract.
