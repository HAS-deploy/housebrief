PMI — home-buying glossary

What it means

PMI stands for Private Mortgage Insurance. It's a type of insurance policy that protects the lender — not you — if you stop making payments on your loan. Lenders typically require it when a borrower puts down less than 20% of the home's purchase price on a conventional loan. The cost is usually added to your monthly mortgage payment.

PMI premiums generally range from 0.2% to 2% of your loan balance per year, depending on your credit score, loan size, and down payment amount. On a $300,000 loan, that could mean anywhere from $50 to $500 extra per month.

Why it matters

PMI comes up the moment you start comparing loan options with a down payment under 20%. It directly affects your monthly payment and your overall cost of homeownership. The good news: PMI isn't permanent. Once you've built enough equity — typically 20% of the home's original value — you can request cancellation. Under the federal Homeowners Protection Act, lenders are required to automatically cancel PMI once your equity reaches 22% based on your original amortization schedule.

It's worth factoring PMI into your budget calculations early. Some buyers choose to wait and save a larger down payment to avoid it entirely. Others decide the trade-off makes sense — getting into a home sooner, even with the added monthly cost.

Example

You purchase a home for $350,000 with a 10% down payment ($35,000), leaving a loan balance of $315,000. Your lender requires PMI at a rate of 0.7% annually. That adds roughly $184 per month to your payment. Once your loan balance drops to $280,000 (80% of the purchase price), you can request removal of PMI and stop paying that monthly fee.

Common confusions

  • PMI vs. MIP: MIP (Mortgage Insurance Premium) is the equivalent charge on FHA loans, not conventional ones. MIP has different rules — in many cases it lasts the entire life of the loan, which is a key reason some borrowers prefer conventional loans with PMI despite the similarities.
  • PMI protects you: It doesn't. PMI protects the lender if you default. You pay the premiums but receive no direct benefit from the coverage.
  • PMI is forever: It feels that way, but it's not. You have the right to request cancellation at 20% equity and automatic termination happens at 22% — as long as your payments are current.
  • PMI and homeowners insurance: These are completely separate. Homeowners insurance covers damage to your property; PMI covers the lender's financial risk.
