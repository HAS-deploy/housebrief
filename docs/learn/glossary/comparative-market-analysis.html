comparative market analysis — home-buying glossary

What it means

A comparative market analysis (CMA) is an estimate of a home's market value based on recent sales of similar properties nearby. A real estate agent prepares it by finding "comps" — homes that closely match the subject property in size, condition, age, location, and features — and adjusting the price up or down based on differences. The result is a price range that reflects what buyers in the current market are actually paying.

A CMA is not a formal appraisal. It's a professional opinion produced by an agent, typically at no cost, using MLS data and local market knowledge rather than a licensed appraiser's methodology.

Why it matters

A CMA shows up at two key moments. When you're selling, your agent uses it to recommend a listing price — price too high and the home sits; price too low and you leave money on the table. When you're buying, a CMA helps you decide whether an asking price is fair and gives you a data-backed foundation for making an offer or negotiating.

It's also useful after a home goes under contract. If your lender orders an appraisal and it comes in low, understanding the CMA that informed your offer helps you evaluate your options — renegotiate, contest the appraisal, or walk away.

Example

You want to sell a 3-bedroom, 2-bath house in Austin with 1,800 square feet. Your agent pulls five recent sales within a half-mile of similar size and condition. Three sold between $420,000 and $435,000; one was smaller and sold for $398,000; one had a pool and sold for $460,000. After adjusting for those differences, your agent recommends listing at $425,000–$432,000.

Common confusions

CMA vs. appraisal: An appraisal is conducted by a licensed appraiser, follows strict regulatory guidelines, and is required by mortgage lenders. A CMA is done by a real estate agent and carries no formal regulatory weight — though a well-researched one is often close in value.

CMA vs. Zestimate or automated valuation: Tools like Zillow's Zestimate use algorithms on publicly available data. A CMA uses an agent's firsthand knowledge of local inventory, property condition, and micro-market trends, making it generally more accurate for a specific home.

CMA vs. BPO (broker price opinion): A BPO is a similar agent-prepared valuation but is typically ordered by a lender or bank — often in foreclosure or short sale situations — and may carry a small fee. A CMA is usually prepared for a seller or buyer client at no charge.
