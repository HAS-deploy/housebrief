title insurance — home-buying glossary

What it means

Title insurance is a one-time insurance policy that protects a homebuyer (and/or their lender) against financial loss stemming from defects in a property's title — that is, problems with the legal record of ownership. These defects might include undisclosed liens, forged signatures in past deeds, errors in public records, unknown heirs who claim ownership, or boundary disputes. Unlike most insurance policies that cover future events, title insurance protects against issues that already exist but haven't yet been discovered.

There are two types: a lender's policy, which nearly every mortgage lender requires and protects the bank's financial interest, and an owner's policy, which protects the buyer. Both are typically purchased at closing. The lender's policy is almost always required; the owner's policy is technically optional, though strongly recommended.

Why it matters

Title problems are more common than most buyers expect. A previous owner may have failed to pay a contractor, leaving a mechanic's lien on the property. A divorce decree may have been handled improperly, leaving an ex-spouse with a legal claim. Public records may contain a clerical error that clouds ownership. Without title insurance, you could be responsible for resolving — and paying for — these issues yourself, even though they occurred long before you bought the home. Title insurance covers attorney fees, court costs, and any covered losses up to the policy limit.

The cost is a one-time premium paid at closing, typically ranging from a few hundred to over a thousand dollars depending on the purchase price and state. In some states, the seller traditionally pays for the owner's policy; in others, the buyer does. This is negotiable.

Example

A buyer purchases a home and moves in. A year later, a contractor who renovated the kitchen for the previous owner surfaces with an unpaid lien of $12,000 — a debt that was never cleared before the sale. With an owner's title insurance policy, the insurer investigates, handles the legal dispute, and covers the cost. Without it, the new owner would have to pay out of pocket to resolve the lien or hire an attorney to fight it.

Common confusions

Title insurance vs. homeowners insurance: Homeowners insurance covers physical damage (fire, theft, storms). Title insurance covers legal ownership disputes. You need both, and they do completely different things.

Lender's policy vs. owner's policy: Some buyers assume that because the lender requires title insurance, they're covered too. They're not — the lender's policy only protects the bank. Only an owner's policy protects the buyer's equity and investment.

Title insurance vs. a title search: A title search is the examination of public records to look for problems before closing. Title insurance kicks in if something was missed or later surfaces. The search reduces risk; the insurance covers what the search doesn't catch.
