appraisal contingency — home-buying glossary

What it means

An appraisal contingency is a clause in a home purchase contract that makes the sale conditional on the property appraising at or above a specified value — usually the agreed purchase price. If a licensed appraiser determines the home is worth less than that amount, the buyer has the right to renegotiate, ask the seller to lower the price, or walk away from the deal entirely without losing their earnest money deposit.

The contingency protects buyers from overpaying for a property relative to its appraised market value. Lenders typically won't finance more than the appraised value, so without this protection, a buyer could be forced to cover the difference out of pocket or lose their deposit if they back out.

Why it matters

Appraisal contingencies become especially relevant in competitive or fast-moving markets where buyers sometimes offer above asking price to win a bidding war. When the agreed price exceeds what an appraiser concludes the home is worth — called an "appraisal gap" — the contingency determines who bears that financial risk. Without it, the buyer is contractually obligated to proceed, meaning they'd need to bring extra cash to closing or lose their earnest money if they walk.

Sellers pay close attention to this clause too. A buyer who waives the appraisal contingency signals stronger commitment to close, which can make a lower offer more attractive than a higher one that includes the contingency.

Example

A buyer agrees to purchase a home for $450,000 and includes an appraisal contingency. The appraiser values the property at $425,000. The buyer can now request the seller reduce the price to $425,000, negotiate a split of the $25,000 gap, or cancel the contract and receive their earnest money back. If the seller refuses to budge and the buyer doesn't want to cover the difference, they exit the deal without penalty.

Common confusions

Appraisal contingency vs. inspection contingency. These are separate protections. An inspection contingency lets the buyer exit based on the physical condition of the home. An appraisal contingency is strictly about value, as determined by an independent appraiser — not a home inspector.

Appraisal contingency vs. financing contingency. A financing contingency protects the buyer if their mortgage falls through altogether. While a low appraisal can trigger a financing problem (since the lender may reduce the loan amount), the two contingencies cover different scenarios and a contract can include one without the other.

Waiving vs. adding an appraisal gap clause. Waiving the contingency entirely means the buyer proceeds no matter what the home appraises for. An appraisal gap clause is a middle-ground option where the buyer agrees to cover a defined shortfall — say, up to $20,000 — but still retains protection beyond that threshold. These are not the same thing.
