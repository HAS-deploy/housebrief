FHA loan — home-buying glossary

What it means

An FHA loan is a mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration, a U.S. government agency. Because the FHA backs the loan, lenders take on less risk, which allows them to offer more flexible qualifying standards — most notably a lower minimum down payment (as low as 3.5%) and more lenient credit score requirements compared to conventional loans. Borrowers pay for this insurance through an upfront mortgage insurance premium (MIP) and an ongoing annual MIP added to monthly payments.

Why it matters

FHA loans come up most often for first-time buyers or anyone who hasn't had the time to build a large savings cushion or a long credit history. If your credit score is in the 580–620 range, or you can only put down a small amount, an FHA loan may be the most accessible path to homeownership. The loan limits are set by county each year, so high-cost areas allow larger FHA loan amounts than rural ones. The property itself also has to meet FHA appraisal standards, which are stricter than conventional appraisals — the home needs to be safe, livable, and structurally sound.

Example

A buyer wants to purchase a $280,000 home but has only saved $12,000 and has a credit score of 600. With a conventional loan, they'd likely be turned away or face a much higher rate. With an FHA loan, a 3.5% down payment on that price is $9,800 — within reach — and a 600 score meets the minimum threshold. They close on the home and pay a monthly MIP of roughly $150 on top of their principal and interest until they refinance or reach sufficient equity.

Common confusions

  • FHA vs. conventional loan: A conventional loan isn't government-backed, typically requires stronger credit and a larger down payment, but doesn't carry mandatory mortgage insurance once you reach 20% equity. FHA MIP, by contrast, can last the life of the loan depending on your down payment size.
  • FHA vs. VA or USDA loans: VA loans are for eligible military borrowers with no down payment required; USDA loans target rural properties. FHA loans have no service or location requirement and are the most broadly available of the government-backed options.
  • FHA loan = government lender: The FHA doesn't lend money directly. You still borrow from a private lender like a bank or credit union — the FHA simply insures it.
