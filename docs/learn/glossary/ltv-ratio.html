LTV ratio — home-buying glossary

What it means

LTV, or loan-to-value ratio, is the percentage of a property's value that you're borrowing. You calculate it by dividing your loan amount by the appraised value (or purchase price, whichever is lower), then multiplying by 100. If a home is worth $300,000 and you borrow $240,000, your LTV is 80%. The lower the ratio, the less risk the lender takes on — because there's more equity cushioning them if you default.

Lenders use LTV as one of the primary measures of risk when deciding whether to approve a mortgage and at what interest rate. A higher LTV signals less skin in the game on your end, which typically means stricter requirements or higher costs.

Why it matters

LTV affects several real parts of your loan:

  • Approval odds — most conventional loans cap LTV at 97%, while jumbo or investment property loans often require 80% or lower.
  • Interest rate — borrowers with lower LTVs usually qualify for better rates because they present less default risk.
  • Private mortgage insurance (PMI) — on conventional loans, an LTV above 80% triggers PMI, which adds a monthly cost until you've built enough equity to drop below that threshold.
  • Refinancing — when you refinance, your current LTV (based on the home's current appraised value and remaining loan balance) determines what options are available to you.

Example

You want to buy a home listed at $400,000. You have $60,000 saved for a down payment, so you need to borrow $340,000.

LTV = $340,000 ÷ $400,000 = 85%

At 85% LTV, a conventional lender will approve the loan but will require PMI. If you could put down $80,000 instead, your LTV would drop to 80% and PMI would not apply — potentially saving you hundreds of dollars per month.

Common confusions

LTV vs. down payment percentage — these are two sides of the same coin. A 20% down payment equals an 80% LTV. People sometimes talk about one without realizing they're describing the other.

LTV vs. CLTV (combined loan-to-value) — if you have more than one loan against the property (say, a first mortgage and a home equity line of credit), lenders look at CLTV, which adds all loan balances together before dividing by the property value. A clean LTV can look fine while CLTV tells a different story.

Appraised value vs. purchase price — lenders use the lower of the two. If you agree to pay $350,000 but the appraisal comes in at $330,000, your LTV is calculated off $330,000 — meaning you may need to bring more cash to the table than you planned.
