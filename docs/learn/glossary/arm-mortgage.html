ARM mortgage — home-buying glossary

What it means

An ARM (adjustable-rate mortgage) is a home loan where the interest rate changes periodically after an initial fixed period. Unlike a fixed-rate mortgage, where your rate stays the same for the life of the loan, an ARM starts with a set rate for a defined period—commonly 5, 7, or 10 years—then adjusts up or down at regular intervals based on a benchmark index (such as SOFR) plus a set margin determined by the lender.

ARMs are described with shorthand like "5/1 ARM" or "7/6 ARM." The first number is how long the initial fixed rate lasts; the second is how often it adjusts after that (1 = annually, 6 = every six months). Rate changes are typically capped—there are limits on how much the rate can move per adjustment and over the loan's lifetime.

Why it matters

ARMs come up most often when buyers want a lower starting rate than what 30-year fixed mortgages are offering. Because lenders take on less long-term rate risk with an ARM, the initial rate is usually lower, which can meaningfully reduce your monthly payment in the early years. This makes ARMs appealing if you plan to sell or refinance before the fixed period ends—you capture the lower rate without ever reaching the adjustment phase.

The risk is straightforward: if you stay in the home past the fixed period and rates have risen, your payment could increase significantly. Caps limit the damage per adjustment, but a lifetime cap of 5% above your starting rate is common—which can still represent a large payment jump.

Example

You take out a 7/1 ARM at 5.75% on a $400,000 loan. Your monthly principal and interest payment starts around $2,334. After seven years, the rate adjusts based on the index. If rates have climbed and your new rate becomes 7.75%, your payment rises to roughly $2,760—about $426 more per month. If you had sold or refinanced before year seven, you never faced that increase.

Common confusions

  • ARM vs. fixed-rate mortgage: A fixed-rate loan keeps the same rate for the entire term—no surprises. An ARM only guarantees the rate for the initial period.
  • Initial rate vs. APR: The low teaser rate advertised for an ARM isn't the APR. The APR factors in expected future adjustments and fees, so it will be higher and gives a more accurate cost comparison.
  • Adjustment cap vs. lifetime cap: The periodic cap limits each individual rate change; the lifetime cap limits the total increase over the loan's life. Both matter when evaluating your worst-case scenario.
  • ARM as risky by default: ARMs aren't inherently dangerous—they're a poor fit when you plan to stay long-term and rates are low. In the right circumstances, they're a rational financial tool.
