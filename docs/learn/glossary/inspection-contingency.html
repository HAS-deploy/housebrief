inspection contingency — home-buying glossary

What it means

An inspection contingency is a clause in a home purchase contract that gives the buyer the right to have the property professionally inspected within a set timeframe — typically 7 to 14 days after the offer is accepted. If the inspection reveals problems the buyer finds unacceptable, the contingency allows them to request repairs, negotiate a price reduction, or walk away from the deal entirely and recover their earnest money deposit.

The contingency is only active for a defined window. Once that period expires without action from the buyer, the contingency is waived and the contract proceeds as-is.

Why it matters

This clause is one of the most important protections a buyer has. Homes can look perfectly fine on the surface while hiding costly issues — a failing roof, faulty wiring, foundation cracks, or plumbing problems. Without an inspection contingency, a buyer who discovers serious defects after signing has little legal recourse and could lose their deposit if they try to back out.

In competitive markets, some buyers waive the inspection contingency to make their offer more attractive to sellers. This is a significant risk — it doesn't eliminate the problems, just the protection. Some buyers compromise by doing a pre-offer inspection (if the seller allows it) so they can waive the contingency with more confidence.

Example

A buyer goes under contract on a house for $420,000 with a 10-day inspection contingency. The inspector finds the HVAC system is at end of life and the attic has evidence of water intrusion — likely $18,000 in repairs. The buyer submits a formal repair request asking the seller to either fix the issues or reduce the price by $15,000. The seller agrees to a $10,000 price reduction. The buyer accepts, the contingency is satisfied, and the contract moves forward at $410,000. If the seller had refused entirely, the buyer could have canceled the contract and received their earnest money back.

Common confusions

  • Inspection contingency vs. appraisal contingency: These are separate clauses. The inspection contingency addresses the physical condition of the home; the appraisal contingency protects the buyer if the home appraises below the purchase price.
  • Inspection contingency vs. inspection itself: The inspection is the actual visit by a licensed home inspector. The contingency is the legal clause in the contract that gives the findings teeth — without it, the inspection is informational only and doesn't give the buyer the right to back out.
  • "Satisfactory inspection" vs. specific repair requests: Some contracts require a "satisfactory inspection" while others require specific written repair requests. The language matters — in some cases, a buyer can't cancel just because they changed their mind; the issues must meet a defined threshold.
