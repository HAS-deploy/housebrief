closing costs — home-buying glossary

What it means

Closing costs are the fees and expenses you pay on the day you finalize a real estate purchase — separate from your down payment. They typically cover services required to process, secure, and transfer the property, including lender fees, title insurance, attorney fees, appraisal costs, prepaid taxes, and homeowners insurance. Both buyers and sellers pay closing costs, though the amounts and types differ significantly.

For buyers, closing costs usually run between 2% and 5% of the loan amount. On a $350,000 home with a $300,000 mortgage, that means roughly $6,000–$15,000 due at the closing table, in addition to whatever you're putting down.

Why it matters

Many buyers save carefully for a down payment and then get blindsided by closing costs. These aren't optional fees — they must be paid before or at closing, and failing to account for them can delay or kill a transaction. Your lender is required to give you a Loan Estimate within three business days of your mortgage application, which breaks down expected closing costs. You'll also receive a Closing Disclosure at least three business days before closing showing the final numbers. Reviewing both carefully lets you catch errors, compare lender fees, and avoid surprises.

Sellers also have closing costs — often higher in total dollar terms — including real estate agent commissions, transfer taxes, and prorated property taxes. These are typically deducted from proceeds rather than paid out of pocket.

Example

A buyer purchases a home for $400,000 with a 10% down payment ($40,000). At closing, they owe an additional $9,500 in closing costs, which includes a $1,200 origination fee, $1,100 appraisal and inspection fees, $3,500 in title insurance and settlement fees, $900 in prepaid interest, and $2,800 for escrow setup (taxes and insurance). Their total cash needed at closing is $49,500 — not $40,000.

Common confusions

Closing costs vs. down payment: These are two separate line items. Your down payment builds equity; closing costs are fees for services rendered. Both are due at closing, which is why people sometimes blur them together.

Closing costs vs. prepaids: Prepaids are technically part of closing costs but work differently — they're upfront deposits for ongoing expenses like homeowners insurance and property taxes held in escrow, not one-time service fees.

Seller concessions: A seller can agree to cover some or all of the buyer's closing costs as part of negotiations. This reduces what a buyer brings to the table but is reflected in the offer terms — it doesn't mean closing costs disappear.
