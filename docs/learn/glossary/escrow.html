escrow — home-buying glossary

What it means

Escrow is a legal arrangement where a neutral third party holds money, documents, or other assets on behalf of two parties in a transaction until specific conditions are met. In real estate, an escrow company or escrow officer acts as that neutral party, collecting funds from the buyer, verifying that all contract terms are satisfied, and then releasing the money and transferring the title to the appropriate parties at closing.

The term also refers to a separate account your mortgage lender maintains after closing. In this ongoing escrow account, a portion of your monthly mortgage payment is set aside to cover property taxes and homeowner's insurance when those bills come due each year.

Why it matters

Escrow comes up in two distinct moments in homeownership. First, during the purchase process — from the moment your offer is accepted to the day you close, you are "in escrow." Your earnest money deposit sits in escrow, protected from both parties until the deal closes or falls through under defined conditions. This protects the buyer from losing money to a bad-faith seller, and the seller from having a buyer walk away without consequence.

Second, if your lender requires it (most do for conventional loans with less than 20% down, and for all FHA and VA loans), you will have an ongoing escrow account for the life of your loan. Your lender manages this account and pays your property tax and insurance bills directly, so you don't have to budget for those large lump-sum payments yourself.

Example

A buyer makes an offer of $400,000 on a home and submits a $10,000 earnest money deposit into escrow. Over the next 30 days, inspections are completed, the loan is finalized, and title is cleared. At closing, the escrow officer collects the remaining funds from the buyer and lender, pays off the seller's existing mortgage, disburses the seller's proceeds, and records the new deed. The $10,000 deposit is applied toward the buyer's closing costs and down payment.

Common confusions

  • Escrow vs. down payment: Your earnest money deposit goes into escrow but is not the same as your down payment. The deposit is credited toward your total funds due at closing.
  • Purchase escrow vs. impound/escrow account: These are two different things that share a name. One is temporary (the closing process); the other is an ongoing account your lender manages for taxes and insurance.
  • Escrow officer vs. title company: They often work together or are the same company, but their roles differ. The title company researches ownership history and provides title insurance; the escrow officer manages the financial exchange and paperwork at closing.
