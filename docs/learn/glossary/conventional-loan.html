conventional loan — home-buying glossary

What it means

A conventional loan is a mortgage that isn't backed or insured by a federal government agency. Unlike FHA, VA, or USDA loans, conventional loans are originated and (usually) guaranteed through private lenders — banks, credit unions, and mortgage companies — and typically follow guidelines set by Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac so they can be sold on the secondary market. Because there's no government safety net, lenders set stricter eligibility standards around credit score, income, and down payment.

Conventional loans come in two main types: conforming (loan amount stays within Fannie/Freddie limits, which adjust annually) and non-conforming (exceeds those limits, commonly called a jumbo loan). Most home buyers are dealing with conforming conventional loans.

Why it matters

This term comes up the moment you start shopping for a mortgage, because your loan type affects your down payment, monthly costs, and even which homes you can bid on. Conventional loans generally require a minimum 3–5% down payment for qualified buyers, but anything under 20% triggers private mortgage insurance (PMI). Once your equity hits 20%, you can request PMI cancellation — a key advantage over FHA loans, where mortgage insurance often lasts the life of the loan. Sellers sometimes prefer buyers using conventional financing because it signals stronger financial footing and fewer inspection hurdles compared to government-backed loans.

Example

A buyer with a 740 credit score and a 10% down payment qualifies for a conventional loan on a $400,000 home. Their lender requires PMI because they're below 20% down, but once they've paid the balance down to $320,000, they can ask the lender to drop it — saving them that monthly premium going forward.

Common confusions

  • Conventional vs. conforming: People use these interchangeably, but they're not identical. All conforming loans are conventional, but not all conventional loans are conforming (jumbo loans are conventional but non-conforming).
  • Conventional vs. FHA: FHA loans are government-backed and allow lower credit scores (sometimes 580+) and smaller down payments, but carry mortgage insurance premiums that can't always be removed. Conventional is often better long-term for buyers who qualify.
  • "Conventional" doesn't mean standard or easy to get: The word sounds generic, but conventional loans actually have stricter credit and income requirements than many government-backed options. A buyer who can't qualify for conventional financing may intentionally choose an FHA loan instead.
