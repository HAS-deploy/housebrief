easement — home-buying glossary

What it means

An easement is a legal right that allows someone other than the property owner to use a portion of that property for a specific purpose. The right is attached to the land itself, not to the people involved — meaning it typically survives a sale and transfers to the new owner automatically. Common examples include utility easements (letting a power company run lines across your yard) and access easements (giving a neighbor the right to cross your land to reach theirs).

Easements are usually recorded in the property's title documents and can be permanent or time-limited. The property burdened by the easement is called the servient estate; if another property benefits from it, that property is called the dominant estate.

Why it matters

Easements show up during the title search phase of a home purchase. Your title report will list any recorded easements on the property, and it's important to understand what they mean before you close. An easement can limit what you build, where you plant, or how you use parts of your land. For example, a utility easement running through your backyard may prohibit you from putting up a fence or building a shed in that corridor. In some cases, easements significantly reduce the usable space on a lot or affect privacy — factors that should influence your offer.

Example

You buy a house and the title report shows a 10-foot utility easement running along the back of the property. The electric company has the legal right to access that strip to maintain power lines. You own the land, you can mow it, but you cannot build a permanent structure there. The easement existed before you bought the home and will still exist for whoever buys it from you.

Common confusions

  • Easement vs. encroachment: An easement is a permitted use of your land granted by a legal right. An encroachment is when a neighbor's structure (a fence, a shed) crosses onto your property without legal permission — it's not authorized.
  • Easement vs. right-of-way: A right-of-way is actually a type of easement, specifically one that grants passage across land (like a driveway or path). All rights-of-way are easements, but not all easements are rights-of-way.
  • Thinking it's a deal-breaker: Most easements are routine and don't cause problems. Utility easements exist on a huge number of residential properties. The key is understanding exactly where it is and what it restricts before you commit to buying.
