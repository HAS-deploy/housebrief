homestead exemption — home-buying glossary

What it means

A homestead exemption is a legal provision that reduces the taxable value of your primary residence, lowering your annual property tax bill. Most states offer some version of it, though the rules—how much you save, who qualifies, and how you apply—vary widely. The exemption only applies to the home you actually live in as your main residence; investment properties and vacation homes don't qualify.

Some states also offer additional homestead protections beyond taxes. In certain states, like Florida and Texas, homestead laws can shield a portion of your home's equity from creditors in bankruptcy or civil judgments. These two types of protection (tax relief and creditor protection) often travel under the same name but work differently.

Why it matters

This comes up most practically when you close on a new home and need to file paperwork with your local tax assessor or county office to claim the exemption. Many counties don't apply it automatically—you have to apply, often within a specific window after purchase or at the start of a new tax year. Miss the deadline and you may pay full assessed value for an entire year before the savings kick in.

The dollar impact varies a lot. In some jurisdictions it's modest—a few hundred dollars off your tax bill. In others, particularly states with strong homestead protections, it can save you thousands annually. Florida, for example, exempts up to $50,000 of a home's assessed value for qualifying owners.

Example

You buy a home with an assessed value of $320,000. Your county offers a $25,000 homestead exemption. Your property taxes are now calculated on $295,000 instead of the full $320,000. If the local tax rate is 1.2%, you'd pay $3,540 instead of $3,840—saving $300 per year. You apply once, and the exemption typically renews automatically as long as you continue living there as your primary residence.

Common confusions

Homestead exemption vs. mortgage interest deduction — These are separate benefits. The homestead exemption reduces your property tax, while the mortgage interest deduction reduces your federal income tax. Both can apply to the same home, but they work through entirely different systems.

Assuming it applies automatically — Many buyers expect the exemption to be applied at closing or by the title company. In most places, you must file the application yourself after taking ownership.

Using it on the wrong property — If you own multiple properties and claim the exemption on a rental or second home, that's typically considered fraud and can result in back taxes and penalties.
