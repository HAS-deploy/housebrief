due diligence period — home-buying glossary

What it means

The due diligence period is a set window of time — typically 7 to 15 days after a purchase contract is signed — during which a buyer can investigate a property before fully committing to the purchase. During this time, the buyer arranges inspections, reviews disclosures, checks title history, and looks into zoning, permits, or anything else that could affect the property's value or usability. In most contracts, the buyer can walk away for nearly any reason during this period and receive their earnest money back.

The length and terms of the due diligence period vary by state and are negotiable between buyer and seller. Some states, like North Carolina, have a formal "due diligence fee" paid directly to the seller in exchange for granting this window. Others simply treat it as an inspection contingency period with slightly different mechanics.

Why it matters

This is your protected window to find problems before they become your problems. If a home inspector uncovers foundation issues, or you discover the addition was built without permits, the due diligence period is your legal off-ramp. Once it expires, backing out typically means losing your earnest money or potentially facing legal consequences. Buyers should schedule inspections immediately after going under contract — waiting even a few days can eat into a short window. Sellers care about this period too, because the property is effectively off the market while the buyer investigates.

Example

A buyer signs a contract on a house with a 10-day due diligence period. On day 4, a home inspector finds evidence of significant water intrusion in the crawlspace. The buyer gets a repair estimate of $18,000, decides the deal no longer makes sense, and notifies the seller in writing on day 7 that they're terminating. Because they acted within the due diligence period, their $5,000 earnest money deposit is returned in full.

Common confusions

Due diligence period vs. inspection contingency: These overlap but aren't identical. An inspection contingency gives you the right to negotiate repairs or exit based specifically on inspection findings. A due diligence period is broader — it covers inspections plus financing checks, title review, neighborhood research, and more. Some contracts have both.

Due diligence period vs. attorney review period: In some states, there's a separate window for attorneys to review and modify the contract terms. This is distinct from due diligence, which is about investigating the property, not the contract language.

Assuming it restarts: The clock doesn't pause if your inspector is delayed or unavailable. The due diligence deadline is fixed from the contract date, not from when you get around to booking an inspection.
