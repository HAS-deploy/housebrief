How to read a school district by the actual numbers

Why the Numbers Matter More Than the Reputation

School districts have reputations that travel by word of mouth, real estate agent pitch, and neighborhood gossip. Those reputations can lag reality by a decade. A district that was genuinely excellent in 2010 may have experienced leadership turnover, budget cuts, and demographic shifts that its reputation hasn't caught up with yet — and the reverse is also true. The only way to actually know what you're buying into is to read the data yourself.

The good news is that most of the data is public and free. The frustrating part is knowing where to look and what the numbers actually mean in context.

Where to Find the Raw Data

State Report Cards

Every state publishes an annual report card for each public school and district. Search "[your state] school district report card" and you'll find the official state education department site. These are the primary source — not third-party aggregator sites, which round numbers, lag by a year, and sometimes use proprietary weighting formulas that aren't disclosed.

The state report card typically includes:

  • Proficiency rates on standardized tests (English Language Arts and math, usually broken out by grade)
  • Graduation rates
  • Chronic absenteeism rates
  • Teacher qualification data
  • Per-pupil expenditure

NCES: National Data for Comparisons

The National Center for Education Statistics (nces.ed.gov) lets you compare districts across state lines and against national benchmarks. This is especially useful if you're relocating from another state and need a reference point.

Local District Budget Documents

Districts publish annual budgets and sometimes financial audits. These are usually on the district's own website under a board or finance section. They're dense, but you only need a few specific numbers — covered below.

Reading Test Score Data Correctly

Proficiency Rates Alone Are Misleading

A district where 80% of students score "proficient" on the state math test sounds good. But if the state set its proficiency bar low to meet federal minimums, that 80% might mean something different than 80% proficiency in a neighboring state with higher standards.

Check whether your state's proficiency standards are aligned to the National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP), sometimes called "the nation's report card." Many states set proficiency cutoffs well below NAEP proficient. If your state does, treat the raw proficiency percentages with caution.

Look at Growth, Not Just Achievement

Many state report cards now include a "student growth" measure alongside raw proficiency. Growth scores track how much academic progress individual students make in a year, regardless of where they started. A district with lower raw proficiency scores but high growth is often doing excellent work. A district with high proficiency scores but low growth may simply serve a wealthy population and coast on demographic advantage.

Growth measures go by different names: Student Growth Percentiles (SGP), Value-Added Measures (VAM), or simply "academic growth index." Find whichever your state uses.

Subgroup Data Is Non-Negotiable

Aggregate district scores can mask huge disparities. Disaggregate the data by:

  • Economic status (students qualifying for free or reduced-price lunch)
  • Special education status
  • English language learner status
  • Race and ethnicity

A district that looks strong overall but shows a 30-point proficiency gap between its highest- and lowest-income students is telling you something important about how equitably it serves all children. Whether that matters to your specific situation is your call — but you should know it's there.

Graduation and Attendance: The Numbers Beneath the Numbers

Four-Year vs. Five-Year Graduation Rates

Districts report both. A high four-year rate is the headline number. Look at whether the five-year rate is substantially higher — if it is, some portion of graduates are finishing on a delayed timeline, which may indicate credit recovery programs masking earlier struggles, or it may simply reflect students who had documented medical or family circumstances. Neither is automatically a red flag, but the gap is worth noting.

Chronic Absenteeism

This is one of the most underused indicators, and one of the most predictive. Chronic absenteeism typically means missing 10% or more of school days — roughly 18 days in a 180-day year. Rates above 15–20% at the district level are a meaningful warning sign. Post-pandemic, many districts saw absenteeism spike and it hasn't fully recovered. Check current numbers, not pre-2020 baselines.

Dropout Rate vs. Graduation Rate

These are not simply inverses of each other. Students who transfer out, enroll in GED programs, or simply disappear from records may not count as dropouts in official statistics but also won't appear in graduation numbers. Look at the "cohort survival" data if your state publishes it — this tracks what happens to a specific class of students from entry to expected graduation.

Financial Health and What It Predicts

Per-Pupil Expenditure in Context

Raw per-pupil spending figures are not comparable across states — cost of living, pension obligations, and state funding formulas vary enormously. Instead, compare districts within the same state, and look at what percentage of spending goes toward instruction versus administration. A district spending 55% on instruction is investing very differently than one spending 65% on instruction, even at the same total expenditure.

Fund Balance as a Stability Indicator

Look at the district's general fund balance as a percentage of annual expenditures. A healthy reserve is typically considered 10–20%. Districts running deficits or with thin reserves are more likely to face layoffs, program cuts, and instability — all of which affect students regardless of test score history.

Reliance on Local Property Tax

Districts funded heavily through local property taxes are more vulnerable to economic downturns in their specific community and are often where funding disparities between wealthy and lower-income districts are starkest. Knowing this doesn't tell you the quality today, but it does tell you something about structural risk.

Teacher Data You Should Actually Check

Teacher Experience and Certification

State report cards report the percentage of teachers who are "highly qualified" (certified and teaching in their subject area) and often break out years of experience. A school with a high percentage of first-year teachers may be churning through staff, which is worth investigating further.

Turnover Rate

This is harder to find but sometimes appears in district documentation or local news coverage of board meetings. High teacher turnover — particularly in specific schools within the district — is one of the more reliable signals of a management or climate problem.

Student-to-Teacher Ratio vs. Class Size

These are different numbers. Ratio includes instructional aides and specialists; actual class size is what a student experiences daily. Ask the district directly for average class sizes by grade level. The answer is usually available.

Putting It Together: A Practical Framework

When you're actually sitting down with the data, work through this sequence:

1. Pull the state report card for the district and its individual schools. Note proficiency rates but immediately look for growth scores alongside them.

2. Check subgroup data for any group relevant to your child. If your child receives special education services, look specifically at outcomes for that population.

3. Find the chronic absenteeism rate for the current or most recent year. Compare it to the state average.

4. Look at the district budget for fund balance percentage and instruction-as-share-of-spending.

5. Search local news for the last two years. Board meeting coverage, budget controversies, principal turnover, and community disputes often surface things no dataset captures.

6. Call the school directly and ask specific operational questions: average class size, what intervention support looks like for struggling students, how special programs are allocated across schools. How they answer tells you as much as the answer itself.

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

Trusting aggregator site ratings uncritically. Sites that assign letter grades or 1–10 scores to schools typically weight test scores heavily and tell you more about neighborhood income than school quality.

Comparing across states without adjustment. A 70% proficiency rate in one state may be equivalent to 85% in another. Always compare within-state when possible.

Ignoring the school-level data inside the district. Districts are averages of their schools. Two elementaries inside the same district can have proficiency rates 25 points apart. If you have school choice within the district, the school-level data matters more than the district aggregate.

Using old data. Leadership, demographics, and finances can change significantly in three to five years. Check the most recent year available for every metric.

FAQ

What's the single most important number to look at? If you can only look at one thing, make it student growth percentiles broken out by income level. It tells you more about what the district is actually doing for students than any achievement measure alone.

Where do I find special education outcome data? State report cards disaggregate by special education status. For deeper information, districts are required to publish annual special education data under IDEA — search "[district name] IDEA data" or ask the district's special education director directly.

Are private schools required to publish similar data? No. Private schools have no obligation to publish standardized test data, student growth measures, or financial information. You're operating on whatever they choose to share.

How do I compare two districts I'm deciding between? Use the NCES district comparison tool, then pull both state report cards side by side. Focus on growth scores and subgroup gaps rather than raw proficiency, and compare each district's metrics against the state average as a baseline.

What if the data seems inconsistent or confusing? Call the district's office of accountability or data department. Most have someone whose job is exactly this — answering questions about published data. They're usually more helpful than people expect.
