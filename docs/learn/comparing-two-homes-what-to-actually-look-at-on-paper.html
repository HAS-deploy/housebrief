Comparing two homes: what to actually look at on paper

There's an entire genre of productivity advice that turns evening planning into a ritual: a 30-minute reflection, three priorities, a Pomodoro template, gratitude journaling, intention-setting. It's not bad. It's also not happening on a Tuesday night after you put the kids down and just want to watch something for forty minutes before bed.

The version that actually survives is 90 seconds. It costs almost nothing in time, and it does roughly 80% of what the long version does — because the long version's value is mostly concentrated in two or three specific decisions, and the rest is performance.

This is the 90-second version. Read it once, run it tonight, and decide tomorrow whether the morning feels different.

What the 90 seconds is for

Tomorrow's morning is mostly a question of: what should I do first, and what should I have ready before bed so the morning isn't a scramble? That's it. Everything else — the deep priorities, the strategic blocks, the work breakdown — belongs in a longer weekly review somewhere else.

The nightly 90 seconds answers three questions:

1. What is the first thing I should do tomorrow morning? (Not "the most important thing of the week" — just the first thing.) 2. What can I prep tonight that will save me 5+ minutes tomorrow? 3. What's already on the calendar that I might forget?

That's the whole exercise. Three questions, 30 seconds each.

When and where to do it

Anchor it to something you already do. Two patterns work:

The "kitchen close" pattern. Right after dinner cleanup, while standing in the kitchen, before you leave for the living room. The kitchen is already "today is winding down" emotionally, and you're already standing. Phones and tablets get used in this position constantly, so adding a 90-second check is frictionless.

The "before phone" pattern. Right before you pick up your phone or remote to start your evening. The 90 seconds is the toll you pay to enter relaxation mode. This works well for people who reflexively reach for screens at 8:30pm.

Avoid doing it in bed. Bed planning is a trap: either you'll be too tired to think clearly, or you'll spin up on something and lose sleep. Bed is for sleep. Plan elsewhere.

The 30-30-30 template

First 30 seconds: Tomorrow's first move.

Open your calendar. Look at the first thing on tomorrow's schedule. Then ask: "What do I need to do before that first thing happens?"

If your first meeting is at 9:30, what's between waking and 9:30? Is there prep? A document to read? A walk you'd like to take? Decide right now what fills the first block of your morning. Not "be productive" — pick the specific first task.

Examples of what this looks like:

  • "First thing tomorrow: finish the budget memo, then breakfast, then 9:30 call."
  • "First thing tomorrow: take the dog to the vet at 8, then I'm at my desk by 9:15."
  • "First thing tomorrow: nothing scheduled until 11. I'll read for an hour before email."

The act of deciding now means you wake up with a starting move already chosen. Morning-you is bad at this decision; evening-you is good at it. Let evening-you do the work.

Second 30 seconds: Tonight's prep.

Look at tomorrow's calendar. Anything on it that benefits from being prepped tonight? Examples:

  • Lunch in a rush tomorrow → make it now or set out what you need.
  • Outdoor meeting and rain is forecast → set out the jacket.
  • Workout in the morning → put the clothes on the chair.
  • Doctor's appointment → put the insurance card and ID in your bag now.
  • Kids' field trip → sign the permission slip and put it in their backpack now.
  • Early flight → check bag is packed, charge the phone, lay out clothes.

Pick the one or two highest-impact prep items. Do them right now while standing in the kitchen, or note them for the next 10 minutes. Don't list 15 things — pick the ones that, if forgotten, would actually cost you tomorrow.

This step is the single highest-ROI part of the exercise. Five minutes of evening prep often saves 15+ minutes of frantic morning, plus the emotional cost of starting the day already behind.

Third 30 seconds: The "what am I forgetting" sweep.

Glance at the next 48 hours on the calendar. Not just tomorrow morning — through tomorrow evening and into the day after's morning. The question to ask: "Is there anything I'd be unhappy to be reminded of at the wrong moment?"

Examples of what this catches:

  • A friend's birthday on Thursday that you wanted to text on.
  • A bill due tomorrow that needs payment.
  • A package arrival window that you forgot was during a meeting.
  • Trash night.
  • A school event the next morning you haven't yet mentioned to your partner.

If you find something, deal with it now — set a reminder, send the text, pay the bill — or put it on tomorrow's first-move list explicitly.

What this isn't

A few things this exercise deliberately doesn't try to be.

Not a weekly review. Reviewing the past week, processing your inbox, updating your long-term goals — all of that belongs in a longer Friday or Sunday session. Trying to fit it into nightly planning is what makes nightly planning collapse.

Not journaling. No reflection on the day, no gratitude practice, no emotional processing. Those have value but they're a different ritual.

Not a brain dump. Don't try to list everything in your head. Three specific questions, three specific answers.

Not optimization. You're not trying to schedule the day perfectly. You're trying to make sure morning-you has a first move and isn't surprised.

Why it works

Three things make this version sticky.

It's short enough to skip nothing. A 30-minute ritual gets skipped on bad nights. A 90-second one gets skipped on almost no nights — you'll always find 90 seconds before the show starts.

It addresses the actual failure mode of mornings. Mornings fail because you wake up making decisions in a fog, not because you lack a quarterly plan. Pre-deciding the first move and prepping one thing eliminates 80% of the morning fog.

It uses evening-you's strengths. Evening-you is calmer, less reactive, has more context, and is less likely to underestimate how chaotic tomorrow morning will feel. Morning-you can't make good decisions because morning-you is half asleep. Letting evening-you choose the first move is just delegation.

David Allen's Getting Things Done has a version of this in the "next action" concept — always know the next concrete action for any project. The nightly 90 seconds is the same idea, applied to your morning specifically.

What changes after a month

A few patterns show up reliably for people who run this for 30 days.

  • Mornings stop having a "what should I be doing right now" moment, because the answer is pre-decided.
  • The number of "I forgot to..." moments drops by half or more, because the 48-hour sweep catches most of them.
  • Evening relaxation feels different — there's a clean break between "thinking about tomorrow" and "watching the show," instead of background guilt that you should be planning.

The third one is underrated. A 90-second deliberate planning moment, paradoxically, makes the rest of the evening more restful, because the part of your brain that was running background loops about tomorrow gets the input it needed and shuts up.

Edge cases

What about days with nothing scheduled?

Most days are like this, actually. If tomorrow has no calendar entries before noon, the first-move question becomes: "What's the one thing I'd like to be done by 11?" Pick it. That's the first move.

What about wildly chaotic days?

Inversely, days packed solid don't need much planning — the calendar already tells you what to do. For those days, the prep step matters most. Lay out clothes, pack the bag, charge the phone, set the coffee. Reduce friction at every transition point.

What about partners and family coordination?

If you live with someone whose day affects yours — kids' pickups, shared car, dinner timing — the 90 seconds can become a 2-minute check-in. "What's your morning look like? Anyone need a ride? Are we on for dinner at 7?" The structure is the same; the participant count is two.

What if I'm already wrecked at bedtime?

Then move it earlier. Right after dinner cleanup is the most common location, but some people do it at 4pm before leaving the office, or right after the kids go to bed. The location is flexible; the practice is the thing.

A short FAQ

Doesn't this just shift work to the evening? It shifts 90 seconds to the evening and saves 15+ minutes of morning chaos. The trade is heavily positive on time alone, and even more positive when you account for the morning's higher decision cost.

What if I think of something after I've finished the 90 seconds? Capture it in whatever quick-capture system you have — a notes app, a piece of paper, a voice memo — and ignore it until morning. Don't reopen the planning session. The point is that the planning is bounded.

Should I write it down or just think it? Writing it down works better. Even one or two lines — "first thing: budget memo, prep: lunch in fridge" — anchors the decision. Thoughts evaporate; written notes don't.

What about Sunday night? Sunday gets a longer session — 10-15 minutes — because the week ahead has more unknowns. But the daily 90 seconds still happens on Sunday night for Monday morning. The weekly review and the nightly plan are two different exercises, even when they touch the same day.

Will this work if I'm not naturally a planner? It works better for people who aren't natural planners than for people who are. Natural planners overdo it; the 90-second version forces concision. People who don't plan at all benefit from having any structure at all, and 90 seconds is the lowest barrier to entry that produces real change.
