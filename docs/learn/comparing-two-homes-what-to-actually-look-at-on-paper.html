Comparing two homes: what to actually look at on paper

When you're choosing between two homes, the gut-feel stuff — which kitchen you liked better, which backyard made you linger — is real information, but it's also the stuff that's easiest to manipulate with good staging and natural light. The paper comparison is where you protect yourself. Here's what to actually look at, in a useful order.

Start with the true cost of ownership, not the list price

The list price is nearly irrelevant in isolation. What matters is what you'll spend every month and every year to own the property.

For each home, build a simple side-by-side:

  • Mortgage payment at your expected rate and down payment
  • Property taxes — pull the actual tax bill from the county assessor's website, not the Zillow estimate. Taxes can differ by thousands annually between two homes a mile apart, especially if one is already assessed at current market value and one hasn't been reassessed in years.
  • Homeowner's insurance estimate — get a real quote, or at least a ballpark from your insurer. A home in a flood zone or with an old roof will cost materially more.
  • HOA dues — and read what they cover. One home's $400/month HOA might include exterior maintenance, water, and a reserve fund. Another's $200/month might cover only a shared mailbox area and have a depleted reserve.
  • Utilities — ask for 12 months of utility bills from the seller. A house with poor insulation or old single-pane windows can cost $200–$400 more per month to heat and cool than a similar-sized, better-insulated house.

Add these up. The "cheaper" home on paper is often not cheaper at all once you're looking at total monthly cost.

Read the seller's disclosure carefully, not just the inspection report

Most buyers treat the inspection report as the document. The seller's disclosure is actually more revealing for comparison purposes, because it's where sellers are legally required to disclose what they know — roof leaks they patched, flooding history, neighbor disputes, permit issues, insurance claims.

What to compare across two disclosures:

Water intrusion history. Any mention of past water in the basement, crawl space, or around windows is a flag. One incident that was properly remediated is different from recurring issues. Ask for the remediation documentation.

Permit history. If a seller added a bathroom, finished a basement, or built a deck, there should be permits. Cross-check what's disclosed against the county permit records (usually searchable online). Unpermitted work can affect insurance claims, resale, and in some jurisdictions, your mortgage.

Insurance claims. Request a CLUE report (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) for each property. This shows claims filed in the last seven years. Multiple claims — especially for water — can raise your insurance premiums and signal recurring problems.

Age of major systems. The disclosure (or MLS listing) often shows the age of the roof, HVAC, water heater, and appliances. Don't just note the age — research expected lifespans. A roof that's 18 years old on a 20-year shingle is essentially a known expense coming in the next few years. A water heater over 12 years old is past average lifespan. Build these replacement costs into your comparison.

Dig into the HOA documents if either property has one

If one or both homes is in an HOA, the financial health of the association is a legitimate factor that most buyers skip entirely.

Request:

  • Meeting minutes from the last two years. These tell you what the board is actually dealing with — deferred maintenance, disputes, pending assessments, litigation.
  • Reserve study or reserve fund balance. This is the HOA's savings account for major repairs. A well-funded reserve (typically 70%+ funded) means less risk of a special assessment. A severely underfunded reserve means owners can be hit with a large one-time charge for a new roof, parking lot, elevator, or pool repair.
  • Any pending or recent special assessments. A $10,000 special assessment on a condo can completely reframe which property is actually more affordable.

Compare the land and location data, not just the property

Two homes at the same price in adjacent neighborhoods can have dramatically different risk and appreciation profiles based on data that's publicly available.

Flood zone status. Look up both addresses on FEMA's flood map (msc.fema.gov). Being in a FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Area (Zone AE, for example) can mean mandatory flood insurance of $1,000–$3,000+ per year, and it affects long-term insurability as climate risk is repriced.

Zoning and nearby development. Check the local municipality's zoning map. That vacant lot next to Home A might be zoned for a four-story mixed-use building. The city planning department's website often shows pending permits and rezoning applications.

School district boundaries. These are not always obvious and can literally change a house's resale value. Verify both addresses on the district's official boundary map, not a third-party site. Boundaries can shift, and a house that was in a sought-after district when the sellers bought it may have been redistricted since.

Walk/commute data. Pull the actual commute time from each address to your workplace at the time of day you'd be traveling, not a midday estimate. A 20-minute and a 40-minute commute is a 100-hour annual difference.

Compare the inspection findings item by item

Your inspector should give you a written report. If you're seriously comparing two homes, you'll likely have inspected both. Don't just read the summaries — go through the findings and sort them into two categories for each property:

Immediate/safety items: things that need action now — electrical panel issues, active leaks, structural concerns, radon levels above EPA action levels, evidence of active pest damage.

Deferred maintenance: things that are fine now but will need attention in the next few years — aging roof, older HVAC, worn weatherstripping, minor grading issues.

Assign rough cost estimates to each item. Use local contractor ranges, not national averages. This gives you a repair and maintenance delta between the two homes that you can factor directly into your offer or your budget.

A common mistake: ignoring the carrying cost gap on a "needs work" home

A home priced lower because it needs updating often gets purchased with the assumption that you'll renovate gradually. In practice, people live with the problems longer than planned, spend more than budgeted, and during the renovation period are also paying mortgage, taxes, and insurance on a home that's less functional than they wanted. Model this honestly. If Home B needs a kitchen remodel in year two, add the financed cost of that project into your total cost comparison, not just the sticker price.

Look at price history and days on market

Pull the listing history for each property. How long has it been on the market? Has the price been reduced, and by how much? Has it been listed before and expired?

This isn't about playing games — it's about understanding context. A home that's been sitting for 90 days in an otherwise active market is telling you something. It might be priced incorrectly, it might have an issue that's deterring other buyers, or there might be something about the property that makes it harder to finance (certain condo buildings, for example, are ineligible for conventional financing).

Also check whether the sellers bought recently and at what price. If they bought two years ago at $550,000 and are now listing at $525,000, they're selling at a loss — that's worth understanding.

Make the actual comparison table

At the end, build a simple table:

| | Home A | Home B | |---|---|---| | List price | | | | Estimated monthly PITI | | | | HOA monthly | | | | Utilities (annual) | | | | Known repairs (5yr) | | | | Roof years remaining | | | | HVAC years remaining | | | | Flood zone | | | | HOA reserve health | | | | Days on market | | | | True 5-year cost | | |

The true 5-year cost row is your list: mortgage payments, taxes, insurance, HOA, estimated repairs, and projected maintenance, minus any estimated equity gain based on conservative appreciation. This single number often clarifies a decision that felt genuinely difficult when it was purely emotional.

FAQ

What if one home has a much lower price but higher taxes? Run the full monthly cost. In some areas, the tax difference alone closes most of the price gap within a few years. Use your actual mortgage calculator with the real tax figure.

Should I compare both homes' inspection reports myself or rely on my agent? Both. Your agent adds context, but reading the reports yourself means you'll ask sharper questions and not miss an item that got orally summarized away.

How do I find property tax history? Search "[county name] assessor property search" — virtually every county has a public portal where you can enter an address and see assessed value, tax history, and exemptions.

What if I haven't inspected both properties yet? You can do most of this comparison before making offers. The inspection is the last layer — it either confirms your paper analysis or adds new data that changes the calculation.

Is days on market always a red flag? Not always. Overpriced homes sit. So do homes in areas with thin buyer pools or that hit the market at a slow time of year. The question to ask is why it sat, not just that it sat.
