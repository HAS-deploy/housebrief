Comps that mislead: assessor value vs market value

There's a particular feeling — call it the news bloat — where you've spent hours absorbing information across the day and feel less informed than when you started. Headlines blurred together. Stories contradicted each other. You can't remember whether the thing you read on Tuesday about the senator was true or it was the rumor that got debunked Wednesday. You're full, vaguely anxious, and unable to summarize three things you learned.

That's the symptom of an unbounded information diet. The fix is the same kind of fix that works for food: you reduce intake, increase quality, and time the consumption so it's actually useful.

This is a practical guide to cutting your news intake by at least half without losing track of anything that matters.

What "informed" actually means

People conflate two different things when they talk about being informed:

1. Knowing what's happening in the world right now. This is the news headline part. 2. Understanding context — why things are happening, what they mean, what's likely to happen next. This is the analysis part.

The internet has made the first one nearly free and the second one harder to find. Most people, when they say "I try to stay informed," actually mean "I check headlines a lot." The result is high-volume, low-context input that produces strong opinions and shallow understanding.

A useful test: pick a topic you've been following in the news for a year. Can you write a paragraph explaining the underlying causes, the main actors, and what they each want? If yes, you've been informed about it. If no, you've been notified about it.

The intake audit

Before cutting, see what you're actually consuming. For three days, write down every news touch:

  • Lock-screen alerts you read
  • News apps you open
  • Social media posts about current events
  • TV or radio news while doing other things
  • Conversations about current events that count as input

Most people clock 90-150 minutes of news touches per day across these channels, mostly in 30-second slices. Almost none of those slices add context. They mostly add agitation.

The audit is uncomfortable because the total is usually shocking. Three days is enough — patterns emerge quickly.

What to cut first

Three categories take up disproportionate time and produce almost no value. They go first.

Lock-screen alerts. Turn off push notifications for every news app. Not "set to important only" — off. The breaking-news alert is designed to interrupt you, and the cost-benefit of being interrupted by an event you can do nothing about is brutal. Real emergencies — weather, public safety in your area — keep their alerts. National political news? Off.

Doomscrolling at transition moments. The 2 minutes waiting for the coffee, the 3 minutes between meetings, the 90 seconds at a stoplight (please), the 4 minutes in line at the store. These add up. They're also the lowest-quality consumption — half-attention, no context, no retention. Replace these with a fixed alternative: a book on your phone, a Kindle, a deliberately boring activity (looking out the window has been undervalued).

Social media news. Specifically: news content delivered through social media. The combination of algorithmic ranking (optimizes for outrage) and short form (no context) makes social-media news the worst-quality news available. If you're going to consume news, go to a news source directly. The same story reads completely differently in a paper than on a feed.

Cutting these three reduces intake by 60-80% for most people. And almost none of what's cut was actually adding value.

What to keep, and how to time it

After cutting, what's left is the deliberate consumption.

One morning digest, 5-10 minutes. A briefing email, a newsletter that summarizes the day's main stories, or a short podcast. The key features: bounded length, contextualized, written in declarative sentences. You finish it and you're done. There's no "next story" pulling you forward.

One deep read per day, 15-20 minutes. Pick one substantial article a day and read it carefully. This is where understanding gets built. The article doesn't have to be from today's news — last week's longform on the same topic is often better, because the dust has settled and the analysis is more honest.

One audio session, optional, on a slow topic. Podcasts during a commute or walk. Not news podcasts that summarize the day — podcasts about a single topic in depth. History, biography, science, craft. The "news" portion of this is incidental; the value is context.

A weekly synthesis, 30-45 minutes on the weekend. Read a weekly magazine or longer piece that pulls a week's worth of events into a single coherent story. This is where the dots get connected. The Economist, the New Yorker's politics writing, a weekend newspaper edition — pick something that does week-in-review well.

Total weekly news time under this diet: roughly 4-6 hours, well-spaced, well-timed. Most people start somewhere between 10-15 hours of touches. The cut is meaningful, and the retention is far higher.

How quality changes when quantity drops

A few things happen when you reduce news intake substantially.

You remember more. When you read one piece deeply, you actually remember it. When you skim twenty, you remember none. Memory needs context and time to consolidate; news scrolling provides neither.

You become a better conversationalist. Knowing one thing well beats knowing twenty things superficially every time, in every conversation. People who've read deeply on a topic add value; people who've skimmed it add noise.

You stop forming knee-jerk opinions. When the bar to having an opinion is "I read three headlines about it," opinions are cheap and wrong. When the bar is "I read a substantial piece about it," opinions slow down and improve.

You sleep better. Removing late-evening news intake is one of the most reliable sleep interventions. It's not glamorous, but it works.

The "but what if I miss something" problem

The biggest fear about cutting intake is that something important will happen and you won't know.

Two truths help.

Truly important news reaches you anyway. A genuine crisis — local emergency, major political event, market crash, war — finds you through your morning digest, through conversations, through every other channel. You don't need lock-screen alerts for things that matter. They self-propagate.

Most things that feel like "missing" are missing the agitation, not the information. When you check headlines five times a day and then stop, the first two weeks feel weird because the agitation rhythm is broken. You're not missing information; you're missing the dopamine cycle of refreshing.

After two weeks, the discomfort fades and is replaced by a noticeable calm. This is the actual indicator that the diet is working: not that you feel more informed, but that you feel less wound up.

A practical week-by-week reduction plan

Week 1: Cut lock-screen alerts. Don't change anything else. Just turn off push notifications for news apps. Notice how the day feels.

Week 2: Cut transition-moment scrolling. When you reach for the phone at a stoplight or in line, put it back. Notice how often this happens — it'll be more than you think.

Week 3: Cut social-media news. Either mute the political accounts, unfollow news outlets on social platforms, or limit social media to non-news content. If you can't separate, take a one-week break from the platform and see how it goes.

Week 4: Establish the morning digest. Pick one source. Subscribe. Read it at the same time every day. Don't open anything else.

Week 5: Add the daily deep read. Pick one piece a day. Read it slowly. Set a 20-minute timer if it helps.

Week 6: Set the weekly synthesis. Block 45 minutes on Saturday or Sunday for the weekly piece.

By week 6, the new pattern is the default. The old pattern feels obviously bad in retrospect.

What about specific roles or industries

Some jobs genuinely require currency in a specific domain. Finance professionals need market news. Lawyers need legal-industry updates. Teachers need education policy news.

The solution isn't more news — it's targeted news. Subscribe to one or two trade publications in your domain. Read them at a fixed time. Treat them as work inputs, not lifestyle inputs. The general news firehose doesn't get you ahead in your specific field; the targeted trade publication does.

This is also a useful test for whether something belongs in your news diet: does it directly affect a decision you'll make? If yes, it belongs. If no, it's entertainment dressed up as duty.

What the budget looks like steady-state

A reasonable weekly information diet, once you've settled in:

  • 7 mornings of digest: ~70 minutes
  • 7 daily deep reads: ~2 hours
  • 2-3 audio sessions: ~2 hours (overlapping with commute or walks)
  • 1 weekly synthesis: 45 minutes
  • 1-2 trade publications relevant to your work: 1-2 hours

Total: 6-8 hours per week, well-distributed, with high retention. Compare that to the 10-15 hours of touch-based consumption most people currently do, much of it in 30-second slices that produce no retained understanding.

You'll be better informed than before, with less time spent. The math is unambiguous.

A short FAQ

Won't I lose my political/civic engagement? The opposite. People who consume less news but go deeper on it vote, donate, and engage at higher rates than people who scroll headlines constantly. Engagement follows understanding, not exposure.

What about during a major election or crisis? Increase the digest duration and the deep-read window. Keep the structure. Crisis news especially rewards depth over frequency — the first hours of any major story are full of bad information, and the people who panic-check the most are the most misled.

My partner is news-obsessed. How do we coexist? You don't have to convert them. Just stop participating in news-as-shared-anxiety conversations. When they raise a headline, ask "did you read more about it?" If they didn't, the conversation tends to redirect.

How do I handle news that gets discussed at work? A morning digest covers 95% of what people reference in office conversation. For the rest, "I haven't read about that — what's the gist?" is a fine response. People are usually pleased to summarize.

What about TV news? TV news rates badly across almost every dimension that matters — context, retention, emotional manipulation. The exception is a single nightly newscast watched as a deliberate session, not background. Most other forms of TV news are best avoided.
