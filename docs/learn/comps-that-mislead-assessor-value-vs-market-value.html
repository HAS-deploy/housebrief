Comps that mislead: assessor value vs market value

Why These Two Numbers Are Not Interchangeable

If you pulled a list of recent sales in your neighborhood and noticed that the sale prices look nothing like the assessed values on the tax records, you are not reading the data wrong. Assessor value and market value measure different things, are calculated on different schedules, and serve different purposes. Confusing them when pricing a home or evaluating a comp is one of the most common and costly mistakes in residential real estate.

Here is what each number actually means, where it comes from, and how to keep them from leading your analysis off a cliff.

What Assessor Value Actually Is

Every county or municipality needs a way to divide the property tax burden among landowners. The assessor's job is to produce a defensible, consistent estimate of value for every parcel in the jurisdiction — not necessarily an accurate one in the real-estate sense, but a legally supportable one that can be applied uniformly.

A few realities that shape assessor values:

They lag the market by design. Most jurisdictions reassess on a fixed cycle — annually in some states, every two, three, or even four years in others. A property assessed in 2021 and not reassessed until 2024 carries a three-year-old opinion of value. In a rising market, assessed values routinely run well below what buyers will pay. In a falling market, they can sit above current prices for months or years.

Assessment ratios muddy the water further. Many counties do not assess at 100 percent of estimated market value. They apply an assessment ratio — 80 percent, 70 percent, even 50 percent in some jurisdictions. The assessed value printed on a tax bill may be deliberately set at a fraction of full value. If you do not know your local ratio, the raw number is essentially meaningless for pricing purposes.

Comparable sales data used by assessors is often thin. Assessors are working with mass appraisal models applied across thousands of parcels simultaneously. They cannot walk every property, note the kitchen renovation, or account for the fact that the lot slopes into a drainage easement. Their models smooth out the individual characteristics that drive real buyer behavior.

Appeals and exemptions distort the picture. Homestead exemptions, senior exemptions, agricultural classifications, and successful assessment appeals all reduce the assessed value for certain properties without reflecting anything about actual market conditions. Two identical houses on the same street can have assessed values $40,000 apart simply because one owner appealed and the other did not.

What Market Value Actually Is

Market value is the price a willing, informed buyer would pay a willing, informed seller when neither is under pressure and the property has been adequately exposed to the market. It is a point-in-time estimate grounded in what buyers are actually doing right now.

Comparable sales — comps — are how market value gets approximated. A good comp is a closed transaction involving a similar property in a similar location that sold recently under arm's-length conditions. The emphasis is on recently. A sale from eighteen months ago tells you what the market thought then, not what it thinks today.

Market value changes continuously. An interest rate jump of 150 basis points can shift buyer purchasing power — and therefore prices — inside of a few weeks. Assessor value cannot respond to that because the data does not exist yet, and even when it does, the assessment cycle may not allow for a revision until the next scheduled update.

How This Trips People Up in Practice

Using assessed value to validate a list price

Sellers sometimes look at their assessed value, note that their asking price is only slightly above it, and conclude their price is conservative. Depending on the jurisdiction and the age of the assessment, the assessed value could be 60 percent of current market value. The logic fails completely.

Buyers make the same error in reverse. They see an assessed value close to the asking price and assume the seller is being aggressive, when in fact the assessment is simply stale.

Running comps without checking what the assessed values in the sold set actually represent

Imagine you are trying to value a three-bedroom ranch. You pull five recent sales and note that the assessed values on those sales range from $180,000 to $240,000, while the actual sale prices range from $290,000 to $330,000. That spread is the assessment lag and ratio combined. If you anchor your pricing to the assessed figures, you will underprice by a significant margin.

The sale prices are the comps. The assessed values are largely noise for this purpose.

Assuming assessed value reflects improvements

A seller finishes a basement, adds a bathroom, and puts in a new kitchen. None of that gets reflected in the assessed value until the next reassessment cycle — and even then, only if the assessor learns about the work, which often requires a permit pull. Using the assessed value to estimate the improved property's worth is unreliable at best and dangerously low at worst.

Geographic variation in assessment practices

Move across a county line and the rules can change completely. Some jurisdictions are sophisticated, frequently updated, and close to market value by design. Others are chronic laggers with ratios that have not been revisited in years. Never assume the assessed-to-market relationship you know from one county applies to the next.

How to Disentangle the Two Correctly

Step one: Find your jurisdiction's assessment ratio. The county assessor's website or the state department of revenue typically publishes this. If the ratio is 80 percent, divide any assessed value by 0.80 to get the assessor's implied full market estimate — then remember that implied estimate may still be years out of date.

Step two: Look at the sales ratio for your specific market. In some MLS systems you can see both the assessed value and the sale price on closed transactions. Calculate the ratio for a dozen recent, comparable sales. That tells you, empirically, how much the assessed values in your specific market understate or overstate what buyers are paying today.

Step three: Build your comp analysis entirely from sale prices. Adjusted sale prices from recent, arm's-length transactions are your foundation. Assessed values do not belong in the pricing adjustment grid. They can inform your understanding of the tax burden a buyer will carry, which affects affordability, but they do not drive the value conclusion.

Step four: Pay attention to assessment dates. When you pull a comp, note when that property was last assessed. A sale that closed six months ago with an assessment from four years earlier tells you nothing useful about the assessed-to-market relationship today.

Step five: Use assessed value for what it was designed for — taxes. Assessed value is genuinely useful for estimating the property tax bill, understanding whether an appeal might be warranted, and checking that a property is not carrying an unusual tax burden relative to its peers. That is its purpose. When you need to know what a buyer will pay, you need actual sale data.

A Concrete Example

Consider two houses that sold within the same month in the same subdivision. House A sold for $385,000 with an assessed value of $290,000. House B sold for $372,000 with an assessed value of $310,000.

If you looked only at assessed values, you would expect House B to be worth more than House A. The sale prices say the opposite. What explains the assessed value discrepancy? Maybe House A's owner never successfully appealed after a prior over-assessment, so they kept a higher base. Maybe House B received a homestead cap that suppressed its value below what a new buyer will actually be assessed at post-sale. Maybe the assessor's model handled the lot or the finish level differently.

None of those explanations change what buyers actually paid. The market said $385,000 for A and $372,000 for B. That is your signal.

FAQ

Can assessed value ever be a useful sanity check on market value? In jurisdictions that reassess annually and target 100 percent of market value, assessed values can roughly track current prices. But even then, treat them as a rough directional indicator, not a pricing tool.

What if the assessed value is higher than the sale price? This happens in declining markets or when an owner has not pursued an appeal they were entitled to. It means the buyer may face a higher-than-expected tax bill in the first year or two — worth factoring into carrying costs — but it does not mean the seller's price is justified.

Do appraisers use assessed value? Licensed appraisers working on financing transactions do not use assessed value to establish market value. They may mention it as a data point, but the value conclusion comes from adjusted sale prices of comparable properties.

How do I find out if my jurisdiction has a significant assessment lag? Pull ten to fifteen recent sales in your target area, compare assessed values to sale prices, and calculate the ratio. A ratio well below 1.0 — say, 0.65 or lower — suggests meaningful lag or a below-100-percent assessment ratio. Either way, the assessed value is not tracking current market conditions closely.
